\begin{abstract}
GPS-derived position estimates underpin the reliability of many networked and cyber-physical systems; spoofing silently degrades that reliability by shifting the measurement away from ground truth with no structural change to the receiving system. We detect this via hardware telemetry: three window-based statistical features (D, G, V), computed per hardware performance counter, capture the execution footprint of a GPS/IMU Kalman filter's (KF) own adaptive-covariance logic on an embedded RISC-V platform -- a channel independent of the position estimate itself. An unsupervised, one-class fusion model over these features, requiring no labeled attack examples, reliably separates spoofed from unspoofed operation across 20 leave-one-seed-out trials. Scored against the post-onset segment of both jump and drift attacks, it reaches perfect recall with zero false alarms (F1 = 1.000); but this standard evaluation cannot see the case that matters operationally: whether the detector stays quiet on a spoofed trial's own pre-attack segment, where a false alarm is exactly as costly as flagging a genuinely normal system. Under this harder, pre-onset stress test, a persistence-aware feature cuts the false-alarm rate roughly 3$\times$ over a naive peak-value feature at no cost to recall, and excluding a single systematically-biased telemetry channel resolves nearly all of what remains (F1 = 0.988). Reliable fusion of hardware telemetry for anomaly detection depends as much on principled feature and channel design as on the fusion algorithm itself.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Networked and cyber-physical systems increasingly depend on GPS-derived position and timing estimates for correct, reliable operation -- autonomous vehicle coordination, distributed sensor networks, and time-synchronized communication infrastructure all inherit their reliability, in part, from the reliability of the underlying positioning solution~\cite{todo_gnss_reliability}. This dependency introduces a specific reliability threat: GPS spoofing, in which an adversary (or, in benign cases, multipath/interference) shifts the received measurement away from ground truth. A GPS/INS (Inertial Navigation System) state estimator with fixed process and measurement noise covariances has no internal mechanism to notice when this has happened -- it simply absorbs the corrupted measurement at whatever rate its Kalman gain allows, and the system's downstream reliability degrades silently, with no structural indication that anything is wrong~\cite{todo_ekf_spoofing}.

Detecting this class of reliability degradation is normally approached at the level of the position estimate itself -- innovation-based statistical tests (chi-squared/NIS monitoring) on the state estimator's own residuals~\cite{todo_nis_detection}. This is effective, but it is a single observation layer: it depends entirely on quantities the estimator itself computes and reports, which is also why recent work has shown that spoofing can be engineered specifically to stay under such a monitor's threshold~\cite{todo_adversarial_kf}. The system we study here (Section~\ref{sec:system}) instead gates an adaptive covariance-inflation mechanism -- a Strong Tracking Filter, used to maintain estimator robustness under model mismatch -- behind a formal innovation threshold, so the adaptive branch only executes conditionally. This gating decision is a data-dependent code path, and its execution leaves a measurable footprint on the embedded processor's own \textbf{hardware performance counters (HPCs)} -- special-purpose CPU registers, present on virtually all modern processors, that passively tally low-level execution events such as retired instructions of a given class or pipeline stalls, at no meaningful cost to the running program. Concretely, the gating branch's footprint shows up as shifts in FP-pipeline stalls, conditional-branch retirements, and jump-and-link retirements -- independent of the estimator's self-reported output. This gives a defender a second, physically-grounded observation of the same underlying reliability question. The distinction is not merely that this is a \emph{second} signal, but that it is a structurally different \emph{kind} of signal: an innovation statistic is a value the monitored software itself computes from the very measurement an attacker controls, so an attacker who understands the monitor can shape their input to keep that computed value under threshold by construction. An HPC reading is not computed by the estimator at all -- it is the processor's own record of which instructions actually executed, which the attacker can influence only indirectly, through whatever effect their spoofed measurement happens to have on the estimator's control flow. This is what ``hardware telemetry'' buys as a detection substrate: a channel outside the attacker's direct computational reach. Prior work has already established this property for HPC-based malware and side-channel-attack detection~\cite{demme2013feasibility,carna2023fight}. We apply the same property to a different problem: sensor-spoofing detection against a state estimator.

We construct three per-counter statistical features from this telemetry (denoted D, G, V; Section~\ref{sec:system}) and ask whether they can be fused into a single anomaly decision by an unsupervised, learned model -- one that never requires a labeled attack example -- rather than a hand-designed, fixed rule; Section~\ref{sec:why-ml} makes the case for why.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We show that an unsupervised one-class model -- evaluated here with two representative approaches, Mahalanobis distance and Local Outlier Factor -- can fuse hardware-telemetry-derived features across multiple counters into a single reliable anomaly decision, without ever requiring a labeled anomaly example at calibration time.

\item We show that a persistence-aware feature -- the longest run of consecutive time windows exhibiting anomalous statistical behavior, rather than the single most extreme value observed -- substantially improves the robustness of this fusion, and give a direct, controlled comparison against the simpler alternative.

\item We give an honest, cross-validated account of detection reliability, including a documented case where one candidate hardware counter carried a systematic bias unrelated to the anomaly of interest, and show how excluding it restores clean detection performance -- illustrating a general principle for reliability-modeling practice: not every available telemetry channel is safe to fuse without individual characterization.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

GPS/GNSS spoofing detection is typically approached at the level of the receiver or the navigation filter's own residuals -- a representative example monitors the accumulated history of Kalman-filter innovations to flag a spoofer capable of tracking the vehicle's true position~\cite{tanil2016kfins}. Recent work has also shown that spoofing can itself be engineered at the physical layer specifically to evade such detectors~\cite{yao2025physicalspoofing}, motivating detection channels that do not depend solely on the plausibility of the externally observable signal. Within IEEE ICC specifically, GPS/GNSS reliability under adverse conditions is an active topic: GPS-denied positioning~\cite{tsikteris2025pioneer} and GNSS/auxiliary-sensor fusion through outages~\cite{qmul2026gnssdas} both address reliability failure modes adjacent to, though distinct from, the spoofing scenario studied here. Side channels have separately been shown to leak security-relevant state even through nominal protections -- encrypted location-based-service traffic has been shown to leak positioning information~\cite{leaking2025lbs} -- direct precedent for this paper's premise that a channel outside a system's primary output (here, hardware telemetry rather than network traffic) can carry exploitable signal about that system's internal state.

Robust fusion across multiple, individually unreliable input channels has also been studied for federated wireless systems, where attention-based weighting learns to down-weight untrustworthy participants rather than exclude them by hand~\cite{zhang2024federated}; we return to this as a natural extension of our own hand-diagnosed channel exclusion (Section~\ref{sec:channel}) in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Supervised ML classifiers have separately been applied to vehicular network attack detection using labeled datasets~\cite{qian2024vanet}; our approach differs in requiring no labeled attack data at all. Our one-class fusion models are themselves standard: Local Outlier Factor compares a point's local density to its neighbors'~\cite{breunig2000lof}, and Mahalanobis distance under Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage stabilizes covariance estimation at small sample sizes~\cite{ledoitwolf2004} -- what is new here is not the algorithms but their application, over persistence-aware hardware-telemetry features, to this specific detection problem. Unsupervised/one-class anomaly detection over hardware performance counters is well established for malware and side-channel-attack detection specifically~\cite{demme2013feasibility,carna2023fight} (Section~\ref{sec:intro} discusses why this substrate resists manipulation by a software-confined attacker), though not previously applied, to our knowledge, to sensor-spoofing detection against a state estimator.

\section{System Model and Feature Construction}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Adaptive Filtering and the Case for a Hardware-Level Signal}
\label{sec:stf-rationale}

We study a 6-state GPS+IMU Kalman filter (KF) on a RISC-V embedded platform, with fixed process/measurement noise covariances as is standard for resource-constrained embedded targets. The \textbf{Strong Tracking Filter (STF)} is a standard adaptive-filtering technique: when a Kalman filter's predictions stop tracking its measurements well, it inflates the filter's state covariance so the filter re-converges faster. Whether that mismatch is occurring is judged from the \textbf{innovation} -- the difference between the measurement the filter predicted and the measurement actually received at that step -- summarized as its normalized squared value, the \textbf{innovation statistic (NIS)}, and smoothed into a rolling-mean form, \textbf{ANIS}, to average out ordinary sensor noise rather than reacting to every momentary fluctuation.

The STF's own job is exactly what makes it unsuitable as a detector on its own. Its purpose is to keep the filter numerically stable: by inflating covariance whenever the model looks mismatched, it prevents the estimate from diverging under a bad measurement, whether that measurement is spoofed or just noisy. But once the filter has adapted and re-converged onto the spoofed trajectory, the innovation-based mismatch that revealed the problem shrinks back down along with it -- the filter's own corrective response erases the evidence a detector would need, just as the attack is under way. A detector that watches the filter's self-reported innovation statistic, or the STF's own recalibration behavior, is therefore watching a signal the filter is actively working to suppress. We resolve this by separating the two concerns: the STF still runs, gated, to keep the filter stable, but detection is instead based on \emph{whether the gate itself fired} -- observed through an independent hardware side channel rather than the filter's self-reported, already-corrected output. This side-channel signal survives the very recalibration that would otherwise erase it, which is what makes it a more robust basis for detection than the innovation statistic or the STF's own behavior directly.

\subsection{Gating the Strong Tracking Filter}
\label{sec:gating}

We gate the STF branch in our filter so that it executes only when ANIS exceeds a formal chi-squared threshold -- i.e., only when independent statistical evidence already suggests the model no longer fits, rather than inflating covariance at every step unconditionally. Gating is necessary for the reason given above: an unconditional STF re-converges onto a spoofed trajectory fast enough to suppress the very innovation-based anomaly a detector would otherwise observe. Because this gate is a data-dependent branch -- it executes different instructions than the unmodified predict step -- its activity leaves a measurable footprint on the processor's hardware performance monitor (HPM) counters (FP-pipeline stalls, conditional-branch retirements, jump-and-link retirements), independent of the filter's own numerical output.

\subsection{Attack Model}
\label{sec:attack-model}

The adversary spoofs the GPS measurement stream, shifting it away from the true position from a fixed onset point onward, with no corresponding change to the (physically independent) IMU stream. We evaluate two attack profiles: a \textbf{jump} -- a large, near-instantaneous constant offset applied to the GPS measurement at onset -- and a \textbf{drift} -- a small, slowly-growing offset applied from onset onward, a harder-to-detect, low-and-slow variant of the same attack class. A third condition, \textbf{normal}, applies no attack at any point and serves as the negative baseline throughout. Each trial feeds the filter 300 sequential GPS measurements, one per time step, with attack onset fixed at the 150th measurement for both attack profiles -- the trajectory's midpoint -- so every spoofed trial contributes an equal-length pre-onset segment (measurements 1--149, still genuinely normal) and post-onset segment (measurements 150--300). We collect 20 trials each for the normal, jump, and drift conditions, one per seed (seeds 1--20); the 20 seeds give 20 independent realizations of GPS sensor noise, and -- critically for isolating the attack's effect -- a given seed's noise realization is identical across its normal, jump, and drift trials, so the three trials for one seed differ only in whether an attack offset is added on top of that same shared noise, never in the underlying noise itself. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows representative raw-GPS-measurement trajectories (ECEF coordinates) for all three conditions from a single seed: the jump attack displaces the measurement to a distant, disjoint region of the trajectory space at onset, while the drift attack diverges gradually from the same starting point as the normal trajectory -- a qualitative preview of why drift is consistently the harder of the two attacks to detect throughout this paper.

\subsection{Hardware Performance Counter Instrumentation}
\label{sec:hpc}

Hardware performance monitor (HPM) counters are special-purpose CPU registers that tally microarchitectural events -- such as retired instructions of a given class or pipeline stalls -- as an ordinary, passively-read part of processor execution, adding no meaningful overhead of their own. This RISC-V core exposes 8 HPM counters that can be configured simultaneously (hpmcounter3 through hpmcounter10); we assigned all 8 to events we expected, from the gated STF branch's internal computation, to differentiate normal from spoofed operation, then empirically narrowed this candidate set to the five described below. Specifically, the five retained RISC-V HPM counters are: floating-point interlock cycles (hpmcounter3), floating-point divide/square-root instructions retired (hpmcounter4), conditional-branch instructions retired (hpmcounter5), integer arithmetic instructions retired (hpmcounter8), and jump-and-link instructions retired (hpmcounter10). Each was chosen because the gated STF branch's internal computation -- a trailing-window mean, a trace computation, and a matrix product -- only executes on the modified predict path, and so plausibly shifts that specific event count when the branch fires. Empirically, all five show a measurable, consistent difference between normal-operation trials and spoofed trials once the branch becomes active under attack, which is the basis for treating each of them as an independent evidence source in the fusion methodology below.

\subsection{Statistical Feature Construction}
\label{sec:feature-construction}

We construct three per-counter statistical features from this telemetry, denoted \textbf{D}, \textbf{G}, and \textbf{V}, that share an identical three-stage construction and differ only in which per-window statistic seeds them. First, a statistic is computed over a sliding window of the counter's own z-scored time series: \textbf{D} is the t-statistic of a windowed ordinary-least-squares slope fit, capturing whether the counter is trending in a consistent direction within the window, self-normalized against its own residual noise; \textbf{G} is simply the window's own mean level, the most direct measure of whether activity is elevated; \textbf{V} is the log-compressed windowed sample variance, capturing whether the counter's short-term variability itself has changed. Second, this per-window statistic is differenced across consecutive windows, so each detector responds to how much its own statistic \emph{moved} since the previous window rather than to its absolute level -- which would otherwise be dominated by each counter's own baseline scale rather than by the change of interest. Third, this window-difference is z-scored against its own value, at the matching window position, across the 20-trial normal-operation baseline, giving a directly comparable, unitless score per counter per window, with a guard against near-zero baseline variance that would otherwise produce numerically unstable scores. Using three statistics that each capture a different distributional property of the counter's behavior -- level, trend, and spread -- rather than a single one is a deliberate redundancy: it protects against an anomaly showing up in one property but not another, and it is what gives the fusion methodology below more than a single vote per counter.

Formally, let $z_c(t)$ denote counter $c$'s z-scored time series introduced above, and let a window of length $W$ end at position $t$. The first-stage per-window statistics are
\begin{equation}
D_c(t) = \left|\frac{\hat{\beta}_c(t)}{SE(\hat{\beta}_c(t))}\right|, \;\; G_c(t) = \bar{z}_c(t), \;\; V_c(t) = \log\!\big(1 + s_c^2(t)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\beta}_c(t)$ is the ordinary-least-squares slope fit to $z_c$ over the window ending at $t$ (with standard error $SE(\hat{\beta}_c(t))$), $\bar{z}_c(t)$ is the window mean, and $s_c^2(t)$ is the window sample variance. Each statistic is then differenced across consecutive windows,
\begin{equation}
X_c^{\Delta}(t) = \big|X_c(t) - X_c(t-1)\big|, \quad X \in \{D, G, V\},
\end{equation}
and z-scored against its own value at the matching window position $t$ across the 20-trial normal-operation baseline,
\begin{equation}
x_c(t) = \frac{X_c^{\Delta}(t) - \mu_c^X(t)}{\sigma_c^X(t)}, \quad x \in \{d, g, v\},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_c^X(t)$ and $\sigma_c^X(t)$ are the mean and standard deviation of $X_c^\Delta(t)$ across the 20 normal trials at window position $t$. These final $d$, $g$, $v$ scores are what the fusion methodology in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} operates on.

Of the HPM counters instrumented on this platform, five (hpmcounter3/4/5/8/10) carry a usable signal traceable to the gating branch; each contributes a D, G, and V score, giving up to 15 raw per-counter statistical scores per trial as the input to the fusion methodology below (Section~\ref{sec:channel} narrows this candidate set further, to four).

\section{Unsupervised Fusion Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Why a Learned Model Instead of Fixed Rules}
\label{sec:why-ml}

A simpler alternative to a learned fusion is a hand-designed rule: pick a threshold for each counter's statistical feature on its own, then combine the resulting flags with fixed logic such as ``flag if any counter crosses its threshold'' or ``flag if a majority do.'' This baseline has two generic weaknesses that motivate learning the fusion instead. First, turning each counter's continuous score into a single yes/no flag before combining throws away information -- how far past threshold a counter is, and whether several counters are only mildly elevated together rather than one being sharply elevated alone -- that a model could otherwise use to tell a real joint anomaly apart from a quirk in one channel. Second, a hand-written rule only combines counters the way its author guessed they should combine, using fixed cutoffs and simple AND/OR logic, regardless of how the counters actually vary together during normal operation. A one-class model instead learns the joint shape of normal-operation data directly from the continuous, multi-counter feature vector (Section~\ref{sec:persistence}), and turns that into a smooth anomaly score: a coordinated, mild shift across several counters can cross its decision boundary the same way one counter shifting sharply can, without either case being hard-coded in advance. This is the sense in which our fusion is \emph{learned} rather than designed, and it is why we adopt it over the simpler rule-based alternative.

\subsection{From statistical scores to a persistence feature}
\label{sec:persistence}

For each of the 5 candidate counters (narrowed to 4 in Section~\ref{sec:channel}) $\times$ 3 statistical detectors (D, G, V) -- up to 15 features per trial -- we compare two ways of collapsing a detector's per-window score sequence into a single scalar feature. The original construction takes the single largest $|score|$ observed anywhere in the trial's window range: a natural choice, but one vulnerable to an isolated noisy window, which inflates the feature identically to a genuine, sustained anomaly. We instead use the \textbf{persistence feature}: the longest run of \emph{consecutive} windows whose $|score|$ exceeds that detector's own threshold $H$. A single noisy window still produces a run of length 1; a real, sustained anomaly produces a much longer run. Section~\ref{sec:results-persistence} gives the empirical comparison.

\subsection{One-class fusion models}

We fit two representative one-class models on the resulting feature vectors, using only normal-operation trials at calibration time -- no attack example is ever used to fit either model.

\textbf{Mahalanobis distance.} We estimate the covariance of the normal-trial feature distribution with Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage~\cite{ledoitwolf2004} rather than the sample covariance directly. With as few as 19 normal trials available per cross-validation fold (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) against up to 15 features, the sample covariance is poorly conditioned; shrinkage substantially stabilized the resulting distances in our experiments and was necessary to get usable Mahalanobis scores at all at this sample size.

\textbf{Local Outlier Factor (LOF).} A density-based method that compares a point's local neighborhood density to that of its neighbors, requiring no covariance estimate.

For both methods, the anomaly threshold is set at the most extreme 5\% of the training fold's own score distribution -- the 95th percentile of Mahalanobis distance, or the 5th percentile of LOF's score (LOF's convention scores denser, more normal-looking points \emph{higher}, so the extreme tail sits at the low end instead). Both are the same underlying design choice -- a nominal 5\% training-exceedance rate -- read off each method's own scale. Section~\ref{sec:threshold} shows this choice sits inside a stable operating plateau rather than at a sensitive cliff edge.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We evaluate with leave-one-seed-out (LOSO) cross-validation: for each of 20 independent seeds, the one-class model is fit on the other 19 seeds' normal-operation trials only, then used to score the held-out seed; every seed is held out exactly once, and no attack label is ever used to fit any fold.

We report two evaluations that differ only in which trials populate the negative (ground-truth-0) class. The \textbf{standard} evaluation scores genuinely normal trials against the post-onset segment of spoofed trials -- the conventional question. The \textbf{stress} evaluation additionally scores each spoofed trial's own \emph{pre-onset} segment (before the attack starts) as an additional held-out negative case: since this segment is, by construction, still normal operation, a reliable detector must not flag it either. The stress evaluation is what exercises the two robustness results below; the standard evaluation alone cannot distinguish between the feature or channel choices compared in Sections~\ref{sec:results-persistence}--\ref{sec:channel}, since both already score near-perfectly under it.

\section{Results}

All results are from leave-one-seed-out cross-validation over 20 independent trials per condition, so every reported prediction is on a trial withheld from calibration. We build up to the final configuration in three steps: first the feature-design choice (Section~\ref{sec:results-persistence}), then the counter-selection choice (Section~\ref{sec:channel}) -- each compared against the natural starting point of all 5 candidate counters -- before reporting the resulting final configuration's headline reliability numbers (Section~\ref{sec:results-final}) and its threshold sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:threshold}).

This is the paper's general reliability-modeling lesson: a fused anomaly model is only as reliable as its least-reliable input channel, and a systematic, sustained bias in one channel is not something the fusion algorithm -- however capable -- can be expected to average away on its own. Telemetry channels intended for a fused reliability model should be individually characterized before being trusted, not assumed interchangeable by default. We adopt this 4-counter set as the final configuration from this point on.

\subsection{Threshold sensitivity}
\label{sec:threshold}

The percentile threshold in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} (95th for Mahalanobis, 5th for LOF) was not chosen at an unmotivated or fragile operating point. Sweeping this percentile on the final 4-counter, persistence-feature configuration shows F1 = 0.988 held flat across a wide range for each method (Mahalanobis: percentile 85--96; LOF: percentile 4--8), degrading only outside that range -- too loose a threshold admits false alarms, too strict a threshold begins costing recall. Both chosen operating points sit inside their respective plateau, not at its edge.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

All results are from a single embedded RISC-V platform; generalization to other microarchitectures with different HPM event sets is untested. The persistence feature and pre/post-onset split assume known onset timing, appropriate for this offline evaluation; a streaming/online deployment would need a rolling-window reformulation of the same feature -- a continuously-updated run-length counter rather than a batch longest-run computation over a completed window -- a reformulation of the existing causal, backward-looking per-window scores, not new detection machinery, though it introduces a latency/robustness tradeoff we do not evaluate here. The channel-exclusion result in Section~\ref{sec:channel} was diagnosed by hand: we traced the residual false alarms to one counter and removed it. The root cause of hpmcounter3's elevated pre-onset activity is not established here; we characterize and exclude it empirically (Figure~\ref{fig:counterbreakdown}) rather than diagnose it mechanistically, and leave a microarchitectural explanation to future work. A learned, per-channel weighting scheme -- as explored for robust fusion in federated wireless systems under compromised participants~\cite{zhang2024federated} -- could automate this step and generalize to deployments where the unreliable channel is not known in advance; we leave this as future work. Only two attack profiles (a large near-instantaneous jump and a slow drift) were evaluated; more sophisticated, adaptive spoofing strategies designed with knowledge of this detection scheme are not evaluated here.

\section{Conclusion}

We showed that an unsupervised, one-class fusion model over hardware-performance-counter-derived statistical features reliably detects GPS spoofing against a GPS/IMU KF, without ever requiring a labeled attack example, achieving F1 = 1.000 under a conventional evaluation and F1 = 0.988 under a harder pre-onset stress test. Two design choices were responsible for this reliability: aggregating each per-counter score by its persistence (longest sustained run) rather than its peak value, and characterizing and excluding a single telemetry channel found to carry a systematic bias unrelated to the anomaly of interest. Both lessons are general to reliability modeling over fused hardware telemetry, independent of the specific fusion algorithm used.
